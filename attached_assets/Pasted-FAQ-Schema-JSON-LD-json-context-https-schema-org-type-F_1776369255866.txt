🪟 GUIDE 4 — HOW TO IDENTIFY YOUR WINDOW OPERATOR (CRANK)
SEO Title
How to Identify Your Window Operator — Casement & Awning Crank Identification Guide

Meta Description
Identify your casement or awning window operator by arm style, link length, mounting hole pattern, and brand clues. Includes measurement instructions and common mistakes.

H1
How to Identify Your Window Operator

Intro
If your casement or awning window won’t open, won’t close fully, or the crank spins freely, the operator is worn.
This guide shows you how to identify the correct replacement.

Step 1 — Remove the Operator Cover
Most covers snap off or are held by one screw.

Step 2 — Identify the Operator Type
1. Casement Operator
Opens sideways

Has one or two arms

Common brands: Truth/EntryGard, Andersen, Pella

2. Awning Operator
Opens upward

Usually a single straight arm

Step 3 — Identify the Arm Style
Common arm types:

Single Arm

Dual Arm

Dyad Arm

Split Arm

Scissor Arm

Step 4 — Measure the Arms
Critical measurements:

Arm length (pivot to tip)

Link length

Offset angle

Mounting hole spacing

Step 5 — Look for Brand Clues
Truth/EntryGard operators often have:

Stamped numbers (e.g., “45054”, “45251”)

Distinctive gear housings

Specific arm shapes

Andersen and Pella have unique mounting patterns.

Common Mistakes
Measuring only the long arm

Ignoring the link arm

Ordering by handle style instead of operator style

Assuming all dual‑arm operators are interchangeable

If Your Operator Looks Like This → Go Here
Two arms with a gear housing → Dual Arm

One straight arm → Single Arm

Two arms that scissor open → Scissor Operator

Short link arm with offset → Dyad Operator

How to Identify Your Door Lock

Intro
If your door won’t latch, won’t lock, or the handle feels loose, the lock or mortise case may be worn.
This guide shows you how to identify the correct replacement.

Step 1 — Identify the Door Type
Sliding Patio Door

Swinging Patio Door

Entry Door

Each uses different lock hardware.

Step 2 — Remove the Lock or Faceplate
You must remove the lock to measure it accurately.

Step 3 — Identify the Lock Style
Sliding Door Locks
Hook latch

Mortise lock

Surface‑mounted lock

Multi‑point lock

Swinging Door Locks
Lever latch

Deadbolt

Multi‑point system

Step 4 — Measure the Critical Dimensions
Faceplate height

Faceplate width

Screw hole spacing

Backset (distance from edge to spindle)

Mortise depth

Latch style (hook, tongue, deadbolt)

Step 5 — Look for Brand Clues
Common brands:

Andersen

Pella

Milgard

Truth

Hoppe (multi‑point)

Common Mistakes
Measuring only the faceplate

Ignoring backset

Ordering by door brand alone

Assuming all mortise locks are interchangeable

If Your Lock Looks Like This → Go Here

Tall multi‑point strip → Multi‑point lock

Short rectangular case → Standard mortise lock

Surface‑mounted latch → Surface lock

🪟 GUIDE 6 — HOW TO IDENTIFY YOUR GLAZING BEAD
SEO Title
How to Identify Your Glazing Bead — Vinyl, Aluminum, and OEM Window Bead Identification Guide

Meta Description
Identify your glazing bead by profile shape, leg height, kerf width, and OEM fitment. Includes measurement instructions and common mistakes.

H1
How to Identify Your Glazing Bead

Intro
If your window glass rattles, leaks air, or the bead is cracked or missing, you need a replacement glazing bead.
This guide shows you how to identify the correct profile.

Step 1 — Remove a Clean Sample
Use a putty knife to gently pry out a 2–3 inch section.

Step 2 — Identify the Profile Type
Common glazing bead styles:

Snap‑in vinyl bead

Kerf‑in bead

Aluminum bead

OEM‑specific molded profiles

Step 3 — Measure the Critical Dimensions
Profile height

Profile width

Kerf width (if applicable)

Leg height

Glass thickness (single, double, tempered)

Step 4 — Match the Profile Shape
Lay the bead flat and compare the silhouette to:

OEM diagrams

Product photos

Cross‑section drawings

Shape match is the #1 factor.

Common Mistakes
Measuring a warped or cracked sample

Ordering by window brand alone

Ignoring kerf width

Assuming “close enough” will snap in

If Your Bead Looks Like This → Go Here
Flat top with T‑barb → Kerf‑in bead

Rounded top with snap legs → Snap‑in vinyl bead

Rigid metal strip → Aluminum bead

Unique molded shape → OEM‑specific bead
